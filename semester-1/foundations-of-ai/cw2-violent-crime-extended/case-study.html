Regularising all 99 features beat every attempt to cut them down

Part 1 found that PCA/SVD dimensionality reduction hurt this crime-rate model. Part 2 builds a Ridge regressor from scratch — gradient descent, no scikit-learn — to ask a sharper question: is the real problem dimensionality, or noise in correlated features? Regularising the full feature set matched the raw model's accuracy and tamed wild, collinearity-driven coefficients, while a second, harsher attempt at feature-cutting made things far worse.

01 · Regularising kept nearly all the raw accuracy

Ridge + gradient descent scored 0.021 test MSE, level with the 0.019 unregularised benchmark on the identical 99-feature split — the two models' predictions correlate at 0.961. Regularisation cost almost nothing in raw accuracy.

02 · Cutting collinear features backfired worse than PCA did

Dropping every feature with VIF > 10 shrank the input from 99 to 57 but nearly quadrupled test error (0.019→0.076). The same "fewer features" instinct that hurt PCA/SVD in Part 1 hurts here too, via a different selection method.

03 · Same feature, opposite sign

Without regularisation, TotalPctDiv's coefficient flips from a sensible +0.05 (ridge) to −0.62 (unregularised) — a textbook symptom of multicollinearity among correlated features that the ridge penalty resolved.

Methods

Ridge, tuned by gradient descent across ~4,000 configurations

A Ridge regressor solved by mini-batch gradient descent with momentum and learning-rate decay (no scikit-learn) was built to test Part 1's conclusion more directly: is the problem dimensionality, or noise in correlated features? Learning rate and batch size were grid-searched with early stopping on validation MSE (winner: LR=1, batch=32), on top of a broader ~4,000-combination sweep over momentum, decay and patience run separately — and the final model trains on all 99 standardised features, no reduced subset.

On the held-out test set, ridge scored 0.021 MSE against 0.019 for the unregularised pseudo-inverse solution on the same features — a gap within noise, and the two models' predictions correlate at 0.961. The real difference is underneath: on shared top-weighted features, ridge's coefficients stayed within about ±0.09, while the unregularised fit swung to ±0.7 on the same features and, for TotalPctDiv, flipped sign entirely — a classic symptom of multicollinearity, later confirmed directly by a variance-inflation-factor check that flagged features well past the conventional VIF>10 threshold. Residuals are visually close to normal but not by a strict Shapiro–Wilk test (W=0.920, p<.001, on n=399), and the model produces a handful of small, physically invalid negative crime-rate predictions near zero — a known blind spot of an unconstrained linear model applied to a rate that can't go below 0. The VIF-pruned comparison model (99→57 features, dropped in order of collinearity) underfits badly, which is really the same lesson as Part 1's PCA result: this dataset's redundancy is exactly where a lot of its predictive signal lives.

Data

A right-skewed target, and a handful of features that actually explain it

The same 1,994-community, 99-feature dataset from Part 1, examined here for what predicts crime rather than how to compress it. The target is heavily right-skewed (skew 1.52, kurtosis 1.83): median violent crime rate is 0.15, yet 44 communities sit at the maximum value of 1 — over 6× the typical community, and disproportionately cities. Two hypothesis tests (high- vs. low-poverty; high- vs. low-% white) both rejected the null at p<.001, confirming real structure behind the correlations below.
