One model, six styles, and a grammar that bends by the numbers

602 short stories, generated by GPT‑5‑mini from a single structured template that only varies theme, setting, country and requested style, turn out to differ from each other in ways that are measurable, not just readable. Word-frequency decay, sentence-dependency structure and lexical fingerprints all shift systematically with the style prompt — enough that a two-feature classifier recovers the intended style 76% of the time.

01 · Zipf's law, bent on purpose

Every style's rank–frequency curve follows a power law (R² 0.91–0.95), but none matches the canonical −1 exponent — no 95% CI includes it. Legalistic prose sits closest (−1.01); children's stories decay fastest (−1.42), consistent with a far more constrained vocabulary.

02 · Syntax stretches and contracts with style

Maximum dependency distance — how far a word sits from its grammatical head — varies enormously by style (Kruskal–Wallis H = 10228, p<0.001). Stream-of-consciousness prose averages a 47.4-token longest span per sentence (one hits 222 tokens), sixteen times hard-boiled's 2.9.

03 · The fingerprint is real, not cosmetic

A logistic regression using only two features — sentence length and pronoun frequency per 100k words — recovers the correct style 76% of the time (macro F1 0.75) against a ≈17% chance baseline, with journalistic and legalistic almost perfectly separated (F1 > 0.88).

Methods

Two parsers, one statistical test, and a classifier as a sanity check

spaCy's en_core_web_sm dependency parser was chosen over the larger transformer pipeline after benchmarking found the latter five times slower with no measurable gain in accuracy on this corpus. Maximum dependency distance per sentence — abs(token.i - token.head.i) — was used as a proxy for syntactic complexity, and a Kruskal–Wallis test (chosen because the metric is heavy-tailed and non-normal) confirmed the six styles differ significantly.

As a further check that these were genuine stylistic signals rather than noise, a logistic regression on two independent features (sentence length, pronoun density) separated the six styles at 76% accuracy (macro F1 0.75) — journalistic and legalistic were recovered almost perfectly (F1 > 0.88), while descriptive and stream-of-consciousness overlapped more (F1 ≈ 0.46–0.48), consistent with their similarly long, discursive sentences. The main methodological caveat sits with sentence-boundary detection: stream-of-consciousness prose has very few full stops, so a custom paragraph-boundary rule was written to stop entire stories being parsed as one sentence, but the longest dependency span found (222 tokens) is still sensitive to exactly where that rule draws the line. Zipf regressions were also fit on lemmas rather than raw word forms — a standard transformation, but not the only defensible one, and it will shift the fitted slopes somewhat versus a token-based fit.

Data

602 GPT-5-mini stories, six styles, one template

Each story came from the same structured prompt template — a theme, setting, country and character role were randomised per story, while the style instruction (e.g. legalistic, stream of consciousness) was fixed within each group — producing 602 short stories of 471–790 words each, with every style represented by at least 85 stories.
